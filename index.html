2.1 Fundamental topics

Pre-procedure clinical reviewSection A · Fundamentals›

Pre-procedure clinical review

  • Confirm the indication yourself and the alternative treatments; EBIR explicitly expects primary clinical responsibility, not merely technical execution.
  • Review recent diagnostic imaging and decide whether additional imaging is required for planning.
  • Focused history/exam: comorbidity, frailty/performance status, airway/sedation risk, allergy, infection, pregnancy where relevant, antithrombotics, renal function and prior procedures.
  • Consent should cover expected benefit, common + serious complications, alternatives, post-procedure course and what happens if the intended procedure cannot be completed.
Haemostasis / antithromboticsSection A · Fundamentals›

Haemostasis / antithrombotics

  • CIRSE Clinical Practice Manual example: platelet count at least 50 × 10⁹/L for IR procedures; correct INR if >2 for low-bleeding-risk procedures and if >1.5 for moderate/high-risk procedures.
  • These are framework values, not a substitute for your hospital anticoagulation policy. Procedure risk, thrombotic risk, drug half-life, renal function and urgency all matter.
  • Bridging/reversal decisions should be made with the relevant treating team when thrombotic risk is substantial.
Contrast & kidney safetySection A · Fundamentals›

Contrast & kidney safety

  • For intravascular iodinated contrast, review renal function and risk of contrast-associated AKI; use current ESUR/local guidance for high-risk patients.
  • Use the lowest contrast volume that still achieves a safe result; consider US, CO₂ angiography or other contrast-sparing strategies where appropriate.
  • Previous hypersensitivity requires mechanism/severity review and a local premedication/alternative-agent pathway—not a generic “contrast allergy” label.
Sedation / anaesthesiaSection A · Fundamentals›

Sedation / anaesthesia

  • Match anaesthesia to pain, immobility requirement, duration, physiological reserve and rescue risk.
  • Deep sedation and general anaesthesia require appropriate anaesthetic expertise and monitoring; moderate sedation still requires airway/resuscitation capability.
  • Plan post-procedure analgesia before starting a painful procedure (embolisation/ablation), not after the patient is already in crisis.
Antibiotics & infectionSection A · Fundamentals›

Antibiotics & infection

  • Most clean vascular procedures do not require routine prophylaxis, while instrumentation of obstructed biliary/urinary systems carries higher bacteraemia risk and often requires prophylaxis/therapy.
  • If sepsis is the indication, obtain cultures when feasible without delaying source control and ensure antibiotics are active before/around intervention.
  • Prior bilioenteric anastomosis or biliary instrumentation markedly changes infection risk for liver embolisation/ablation.
Radiation & occupational safetySection A · Fundamentals›

Radiation & occupational safety

  • Use time, distance and shielding deliberately; collimate, lower pulse/frame rates, use last-image hold and avoid unnecessary magnification.
  • Optimise geometry: detector close to patient, x-ray tube as far as practical; be especially cautious with steep obliques and lateral projections.
  • Track dose during prolonged procedures and follow your institutional patient-dose follow-up threshold. Occupational eye/hand protection and dosimetry are syllabus topics.
Access & bailout planningSection A · Fundamentals›

Access & bailout planning

  • Ultrasound-guided vascular access should be default where feasible.
  • Before puncture, decide the access vessel, sheath size, closure method and how you will control bleeding if the vessel ruptures.
  • For any embolisation: identify the target, dangerous collaterals, desired endpoint and what embolic behaviour you need (temporary/permanent, proximal/distal, liquid/particle/coil/plug).
Modified CIRSE complication classificationSection A · Fundamentals›

Modified CIRSE complication classification

  • 1a: complication resolved during the same procedure and intended therapy completed. 1b: resolved during the same procedure but intended therapy not completed.
  • 2: unplanned prolonged hospitalisation <48 h without additional therapy.
  • 3a: additional therapy and/or hospitalisation >48 h but <2 weeks, with no permanent sequelae. 3b: hospitalisation >2 weeks, no permanent sequelae.
  • 4: mild permanent sequelae; 5: severe sequelae requiring assistance in daily life; 6: death.
  • Record timing (intra-, peri- or delayed; late >30 days) and the actual patient impact.

2.2.1 Vascular diagnosis & intervention

Lower-limb angioplasty / stentingB · Vascular · 2.2.1.1.1 · HIGH-YIELD›
Endovascular revascularisation for lifestyle-limiting PAD or chronic limb-threatening ischaemia after clinical, haemodynamic and anatomic assessment.

Indications

  • Chronic limb-threatening ischaemia (rest pain, tissue loss) when revascularisation is appropriate.
  • Lifestyle-limiting claudication despite best medical therapy/exercise in selected patients.
  • Focal or multilevel stenotic/occlusive disease suitable for an endovascular strategy.

Contraindications / cautions

  • No meaningful clinical indication or symptoms explained by non-vascular disease.
  • Uncorrectable severe coagulopathy or uncontrolled sepsis at the access/treatment site.
  • Caution: severe renal dysfunction, contrast allergy, poor distal runoff, no viable limb, inability to tolerate antithrombotic therapy.

Work-up

  • History: walking distance, rest pain, wounds, smoking, diabetes, cardiovascular risk, prior bypass/stents.
  • Exam: pulses, wounds/infection, capillary refill; ABI/TBI where reliable.
  • Duplex and/or CTA/MRA for anatomy; assess inflow, target lesion, runoff and bypass options.
  • FBC, renal function, coagulation as clinically indicated; reconcile antiplatelet/anticoagulant therapy.

Planning & technique

  • Plan access (ipsilateral antegrade, contralateral crossover, radial/brachial or pedal in selected cases).
  • Cross lesion intraluminally where feasible; subintimal recanalisation is often appropriate for CTOs.
  • Balloon sizing to reference vessel; stent for recoil, flow-limiting dissection, residual stenosis or selected primary-stent territories.
  • Completion angiography: brisk inline flow, no significant residual stenosis, embolus, dissection or perforation.

Immediate post-care

  • Access-site and limb neurovascular observations; analgesia and hydration as appropriate.
  • Best medical therapy: smoking cessation, lipid/diabetes/BP control, antithrombotic regimen per local vascular protocol.
  • Wound/podiatry pathway for CLTI.

Follow-up

  • Clinical + pulse/wound review; ABI/TBI or duplex according to territory/device and local surveillance pathway.
  • Escalate early for recurrent symptoms, falling ABI, tissue deterioration or suspected stent failure.

Complications & rescue

  • Access bleeding/pseudoaneurysm → compression, thrombin injection, covered stent or surgery as required.
  • Distal embolisation / acute thrombosis → aspiration/thrombectomy, lysis or embolic retrieval; restore runoff.
  • Perforation → prolonged balloon tamponade ± covered stent/embolisation.
  • Contrast-associated AKI, cholesterol embolisation, dissection, compartment syndrome.

EBIR pearls

  • EBIR answers should start with clinical indication and best medical therapy, not the device.
  • Always describe inflow + lesion + runoff and have a bailout plan before crossing.
Acute limb ischaemia: thrombectomy / thrombolysisB · Vascular · 2.2.1.1.1 · HIGH-YIELD · ON-CALL›
Urgent limb-salvage pathway integrating Rutherford severity, embolic vs thrombotic cause, anticoagulation and revascularisation.

Indications

  • Rutherford I–IIa: endovascular thrombolysis/thrombectomy often appropriate depending on cause and anatomy.
  • Selected IIb limbs may undergo rapid percutaneous thrombectomy when reperfusion can be achieved immediately; surgical input is essential.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Irreversible limb (Rutherford III) → revascularisation is generally inappropriate and potentially harmful.
  • Major absolute/relative thrombolysis contraindications if lytic therapy is contemplated; mechanical options may remain possible.
  • Caution: prolonged ischaemia, renal failure, severe reperfusion risk.

Work-up

  • Immediate limb exam: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paraesthesia, paralysis, temperature; document Doppler signals.
  • IV heparin unless contraindicated; analgesia, resuscitation, urgent vascular/IR coordination.
  • CTA if it will not delay necessary treatment; identify embolus, thrombosed aneurysm/stent/bypass and distal runoff.
  • FBC/coagulation/fibrinogen/renal function and group & screen when time permits.

Planning & technique

  • Choose aspiration/mechanical thrombectomy, catheter-directed lysis, angioplasty/stenting or hybrid strategy.
  • Treat the underlying lesion after thrombus clearance.
  • Completion assessment must include distal runoff and clinical reperfusion, not just an angiographic lumen.

Immediate post-care

  • Monitor limb, access site, haemoglobin/coagulation if lysis, renal function and systemic reperfusion markers.
  • Watch for compartment syndrome and hyperkalaemia/acidosis after prolonged ischaemia.

Follow-up

  • Determine aetiology (AF/embolism, PAD, aneurysm, graft/stent failure, thrombophilia where appropriate).
  • Secondary prevention and surveillance of treated lesion/graft.

Complications & rescue

  • Major bleeding / intracranial haemorrhage with thrombolysis → stop lytic, urgent resuscitation and specialist reversal pathway.
  • Distal embolisation or no-reflow → aspiration, local lysis, vasodilator or further revascularisation.
  • Reperfusion injury/compartment syndrome → urgent surgical fasciotomy assessment.

EBIR pearls

  • The key first question is Rutherford class: viable, threatened or irreversible?
  • For IIb, speed matters more than elegance.
EVAR / TEVARB · Vascular · 2.2.1.1.3 · HIGH-YIELD›
Endograft treatment of aortic aneurysmal and selected acute aortic syndromes with anatomy-driven planning and lifelong surveillance.

Indications

  • Aneurysm/dissection/traumatic aortic injury meeting guideline- and patient-specific intervention criteria.
  • Complicated type B dissection (e.g. rupture, malperfusion, refractory pain/hypertension) generally favours intervention.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Anatomy incompatible with safe proximal/distal seal or access without a feasible adjunctive strategy.
  • Uncorrectable infection/mycotic process is a major concern for standard endografting.
  • Caution: renal impairment, hostile access, short/angulated neck, branch-vessel coverage, connective tissue disease.

Work-up

  • Thin-slice arterial CTA from relevant supra-aortic/visceral branches through femoral access vessels.
  • Measure landing zones, diameters, angulation, thrombus/calcification, branch origins and access calibre.
  • FBC, renal function, coagulation; cardiac/anaesthetic risk; assess need for CSF/spinal protection in extensive thoracic coverage.

Planning & technique

  • Ultrasound-guided access; pre-close where appropriate; confirm device orientation and landing zones.
  • Preserve/plan branch perfusion; consider debranching/fenestrated/branched strategy when needed.
  • Completion angiography: seal, branch patency, limb patency, endoleak assessment.

Immediate post-care

  • Haemodynamic, access and limb monitoring; renal function.
  • After TEVAR/extensive coverage: frequent neurological/leg examination and spinal-cord-ischaemia pathway.

Follow-up

  • Cross-sectional or duplex-based surveillance per device/local protocol for migration, sac size and endoleak.
  • Differentiate endoleak type before treatment; type I/III generally demand repair, type II management depends on sac behaviour.

Complications & rescue

  • Type I/III endoleak → ballooning, extension cuffs, relining or branch/adjunctive repair.
  • Limb occlusion → thrombectomy/relining.
  • Access rupture → balloon control/covered stent/surgery.
  • Spinal cord ischaemia, stroke, renal injury, bowel/limb ischaemia, infection.

EBIR pearls

  • Know the endoleak types and which ones are high-pressure failures.
  • TEVAR viva: explicitly mention left subclavian and spinal cord strategy.
Carotid artery stentingB · Vascular · 2.2.1.1.4›
Selected carotid revascularisation using cerebral protection, careful antiplatelet planning and peri-procedural neurological monitoring.

Indications

  • Symptomatic or selected asymptomatic carotid stenosis when stenting is favoured over endarterectomy based on anatomy, comorbidity and MDT assessment.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Inability to safely use required antiplatelet therapy.
  • Unfavourable arch/lesion anatomy, heavy calcification or thrombus may favour surgery/medical therapy.
  • Caution: marked bradyarrhythmia, recent large infarct/haemorrhagic risk.

Work-up

  • Neurological history/exam; quantify stenosis on duplex/CTA/MRA; evaluate intracranial circulation and arch anatomy.
  • DAPT plan, FBC/coagulation/renal function; review blood pressure and cardiac status.

Planning & technique

  • Use embolic protection when appropriate; minimise catheter/wire manipulation in arch and lesion.
  • Predilate selectively; deploy appropriately sized self-expanding stent; post-dilate only as needed.
  • Monitor HR/BP and neurological status throughout.

Immediate post-care

  • Close neurological and haemodynamic observation; treat hypotension/bradycardia.
  • Continue antiplatelet therapy per carotid protocol.

Follow-up

  • Clinical/neurological review and duplex surveillance for restenosis.

Complications & rescue

  • Embolic stroke → immediate stroke pathway; intracranial imaging and thrombectomy where appropriate.
  • Hyperperfusion syndrome/ICH → urgent BP control and neurocritical care.
  • Bradycardia/hypotension from carotid sinus stimulation; access complications.

EBIR pearls

  • Patient selection versus endarterectomy is the core exam issue.
  • Preventing embolisation is more important than achieving a perfect angiographic waist.
Mechanical thrombectomy for strokeB · Vascular · 2.2.1.1.5 · ON-CALL›
Syllabus-level stroke thrombectomy knowledge for body IR services that support neurointervention.

Indications

  • Eligible large-vessel occlusion based on clinical severity, vascular imaging, time window and tissue imaging according to current stroke protocol.

Contraindications / cautions

  • No treatable occlusion or imaging/clinical scenario where procedural risk outweighs expected benefit.
  • Do not delay reperfusion therapy for non-essential testing.

Work-up

  • Activate stroke team; NIHSS/last-known-well; non-contrast CT ± CTA and perfusion/tissue selection according to pathway.
  • Check anticoagulation, platelets/glucose/renal function only insofar as they do not cause avoidable delay.

Planning & technique

  • Fast, atraumatic access; aspiration and/or stent-retriever strategy.
  • Aim for rapid complete reperfusion while limiting passes and distal embolisation.

Immediate post-care

  • Neurocritical/stroke unit monitoring; BP targets and post-procedure imaging per stroke service.

Follow-up

  • Secondary stroke aetiology work-up and prevention.

Complications & rescue

  • ICH/SAH, vessel perforation/dissection, distal embolisation, access complications.

EBIR pearls

  • Time-to-reperfusion is a treatment variable.
  • Know TICI conceptually and the importance of first-pass effect.
Vascular malformation embolotherapyB · Vascular · 2.2.1.1.6›
Flow- and anatomy-based treatment of vascular malformations using embolic/sclerosant agents within a multidisciplinary plan.

Indications

  • Pain, bleeding, functional compromise, ulceration, high-output effects or pre-operative devascularisation in appropriately classified malformations.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Incorrect lesion classification or no clear therapeutic endpoint.
  • Caution: lesions adjacent to critical nerves/skin, coagulopathy such as localised intravascular coagulopathy in venous malformations.

Work-up

  • Clinical exam including skin, limb overgrowth and functional symptoms.
  • US + MRI for low-flow lesions; CTA/angiography for high-flow anatomy as indicated.
  • FBC/coagulation; consider fibrinogen/D-dimer in extensive venous malformations.

Planning & technique

  • Treat the nidus/pathologic compartment, not merely proximal feeders.
  • Choose sclerosant/embolus based on flow and target; protect non-target arteries, skin and nerves.
  • Stage large lesions rather than pursuing unsafe single-session eradication.

Immediate post-care

  • Analgesia, swelling/airway or compartment monitoring where relevant; thrombosis prophylaxis strategy in selected venous malformations.

Follow-up

  • Clinical + imaging assessment; repeat staged treatment if symptoms/target persist.

Complications & rescue

  • Skin necrosis, nerve injury, thrombosis/PE, haemoglobinuria with some sclerosants, non-target embolisation, cardiopulmonary collapse.

EBIR pearls

  • First classify: tumour vs malformation, then high-flow vs low-flow.
  • For AVMs, proximal feeder occlusion alone invites recruitment.
Trauma / pelvic arterial embolisationB · Vascular · 2.2.1.1.7 · HIGH-YIELD · ON-CALL›
Damage-control transarterial haemorrhage management integrated with resuscitation, surgery and trauma imaging.

Indications

  • Active arterial haemorrhage, pseudoaneurysm, AV fistula or ongoing bleeding in a territory amenable to embolisation.
  • Pelvic fracture with arterial bleeding or persistent haemodynamic concern after appropriate trauma resuscitation.

Contraindications / cautions

  • No absolute contraindication when life-saving; coagulopathy/renal impairment are managed in parallel rather than used to deny necessary embolisation.

Work-up

  • Parallel massive-haemorrhage resuscitation; review arterial-phase CT and clinical bleeding source.
  • FBC/coagulation/fibrinogen, group & crossmatch, renal function if time permits.
  • Agree damage-control endpoint with trauma/surgical team.

Planning & technique

  • Fast access; target selective embolisation when stable and anatomy permits, broader empiric embolisation when shock demands speed.
  • Choose temporary vs permanent embolic according to vessel, injury and collateral supply.
  • Always perform a final angiographic survey for additional bleeding and collateral sources.

Immediate post-care

  • ICU/trauma monitoring, haemoglobin/coagulation, transfusion response, access site and organ ischaemia.

Follow-up

  • Re-image for recurrent bleeding, falling Hb or new organ dysfunction.

Complications & rescue

  • Non-target ischaemia/necrosis, rebleeding, access injury, contrast injury.
  • Pelvic embolisation: gluteal/skin/organ ischaemia and sexual dysfunction are uncommon but recognised.

EBIR pearls

  • In shock, the correct answer often prioritises speed and physiology over superselectivity.
  • Negative angiography does not exclude intermittent bleeding—use CT/anatomic clues.
Visceral haemorrhage embolisationB · Vascular · 2.2.1.1.8 · HIGH-YIELD · ON-CALL›
Endovascular control of upper/lower GI, postoperative, pancreatic, renal and other visceral arterial bleeding.

Indications

  • Active extravasation, pseudoaneurysm, arterial fistula or suspected arterial bleeding after failed/unsuitable endoscopic or surgical management.

Contraindications / cautions

  • No absolute contraindication in life-threatening bleeding. Caution with severe diffuse vasculopathy or when embolisation would sacrifice an end-artery without acceptable collateral supply.

Work-up

  • Resuscitate first; review multiphase CTA for site and vessel.
  • FBC/coagulation/fibrinogen, renal function, crossmatch; correct coagulopathy in parallel if possible.
  • Understand prior surgery and altered arterial anatomy.

Planning & technique

  • Catheterise culprit territory; use superselective microcatheter when stable.
  • For pseudoaneurysm with accessible inflow/outflow, “sandwich” embolisation prevents retrograde collateral filling.
  • Empiric embolisation can be appropriate when CTA/endoscopy localises a lesion but angiography is negative.

Immediate post-care

  • Monitor Hb, vitals, pain, lactate and organ-specific ischaemia; resume antithrombotics when haemostasis permits.

Follow-up

  • Repeat CTA/angiography if recurrent haemorrhage. Treat underlying cause (ulcer, tumour, pancreatitis, iatrogenic injury).

Complications & rescue

  • Bowel/solid-organ ischaemia or infarction, non-target embolisation, recurrent bleeding, coil migration, access injury.

EBIR pearls

  • Pseudoaneurysm: think inflow + outflow, not simply packing the sac.
  • In GI bleeding, prior endoscopic clips can be valuable fluoroscopic landmarks.
Bronchial artery embolisationB · Vascular · 2.2.1.1.8 · HIGH-YIELD · ON-CALL›
Life-saving treatment of significant haemoptysis with meticulous search for bronchial and non-bronchial systemic supply.

Indications

  • Life-threatening or recurrent clinically significant haemoptysis with a systemic arterial source.

Contraindications / cautions

  • No absolute contraindication in life-threatening haemoptysis.
  • Caution: unsafe spinal arterial supply arising from the target branch; severe contrast reaction/renal dysfunction balanced against urgency.

Work-up

  • Airway and haemodynamic stabilisation; identify bleeding side if possible.
  • CTA chest to map hypertrophied bronchial and non-bronchial systemic arteries, culprit lesion and pulmonary arterial causes.
  • FBC/coagulation/crossmatch; treat infection/coagulopathy in parallel.

Planning & technique

  • Interrogate bronchial and relevant non-bronchial systemic arteries.
  • Superselect beyond spinal branches; avoid particulate sizes/agents associated with unsafe passage into critical collaterals according to local SOP.
  • Embolise abnormal hypervascularity, shunting, aneurysm or extravasation to stasis/near-stasis.

Immediate post-care

  • Monitor respiratory status, chest pain, dysphagia and neurological symptoms.

Follow-up

  • Treat underlying disease; recurrence warrants repeat CTA/angiography and search for missed non-bronchial supply.

Complications & rescue

  • Spinal cord ischaemia is the feared complication → stop immediately, urgent neuro assessment/supportive pathway.
  • Non-target oesophageal/bronchial ischaemia, stroke (rare), chest pain/dysphagia, recurrence.

EBIR pearls

  • Before injecting, actively look for spinal supply.
  • Recurrent haemoptysis often reflects collateral recruitment or untreated non-bronchial systemic arteries.
Uterine artery embolisation (fibroids)B · Vascular · 2.2.1.1.9 · HIGH-YIELD›
Uterine-preserving treatment of symptomatic leiomyomata with pre-procedure gynaecologic assessment and post-embolisation care.

Indications

  • Bulk symptoms and/or heavy menstrual bleeding from fibroids in an appropriately counselled patient.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Pregnancy, active pelvic infection, suspected uterine/adnexal malignancy.
  • Relative: asymptomatic fibroids, severe contrast allergy, uncorrectable coagulopathy; fertility goals require nuanced counselling.

Work-up

  • Gynaecology review and up-to-date cervical/endometrial assessment as clinically indicated.
  • Pelvic MRI (or high-quality ultrasound) to confirm fibroids, exclude alternative pathology and assess adenomyosis/pedunculated morphology.
  • FBC, pregnancy test, renal/coagulation testing as appropriate.

Planning & technique

  • Bilateral uterine artery catheterisation; embolise fibroid vascularity to accepted angiographic endpoint while avoiding cervicovaginal/ovarian non-target embolisation.

Immediate post-care

  • Aggressive multimodal analgesia/antiemetic plan; monitor post-embolisation syndrome, urinary retention and infection.

Follow-up

  • Clinical review around 1–3 months; MRI/US if symptoms persist or local protocol requires.

Complications & rescue

  • Post-embolisation syndrome, infection/endometritis, fibroid expulsion, transient/permanent amenorrhoea, non-target ovarian embolisation, rare uterine necrosis.

EBIR pearls

  • MRI is valuable because not every “fibroid symptom” is a fibroid problem.
  • Consent must include the possibility of amenorrhoea/ovarian dysfunction and fibroid expulsion.
Prostatic artery embolisationB · Vascular · 2.2.1.2 · HIGH-YIELD›
Endovascular BPH therapy requiring urologic selection, pelvic arterial mapping and careful non-target protection.

Indications

  • Moderate/severe lower urinary tract symptoms due to BPH with inadequate/undesired medical therapy and preference or risk profile favouring PAE.
  • Selected catheter-dependent retention or haematuria related to BPH.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Prostate cancer or alternative pathology not adequately evaluated.
  • Severe arterial anatomy precluding safe catheterisation; active UTI; uncorrectable coagulopathy.

Work-up

  • Urology assessment: symptom score, QoL, PSA/DRE ± further cancer work-up, prostate volume, flow/PVR as applicable.
  • CTA/MRA or cone-beam CT strategy for pelvic arterial anatomy; renal/coagulation/FBC and urine infection assessment.

Planning & technique

  • Superselect prostatic arteries, confirm territory with angiography/CBCT when available.
  • Protect rectal, bladder and penile collaterals; slow embolisation to near-stasis using locally accepted particle strategy.
  • Treat bilaterally where feasible.

Immediate post-care

  • Analgesia/NSAID if appropriate, urinary symptoms, dysuria/haematuria, infection and retention monitoring.

Follow-up

  • Symptom score, flow/PVR and medication/catheter status at interval urologic/IR follow-up.

Complications & rescue

  • Post-embolisation pelvic pain/dysuria, UTI, urinary retention, haematuria/haematospermia.
  • Non-target rectal/bladder/penile ischaemia is the key preventable complication.

EBIR pearls

  • PAE is a clinical service, not just bilateral embolisation: exclude cancer and quantify symptoms.
  • CBCT can be more valuable for avoiding non-target embolisation than for showing off anatomy.
Priapism embolisationB · Vascular · 2.2.1.2.1›
Selective embolisation for high-flow (non-ischaemic) priapism after differentiating it from the urologic emergency of low-flow priapism.

Indications

  • Persistent high-flow priapism with arterial-cavernosal fistula/pseudoaneurysm after trauma or iatrogenic injury, especially if conservative management fails.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Low-flow/ischaemic priapism → urgent urologic aspiration/alpha-agonist pathway, not arterial embolisation.

Work-up

  • History: trauma, pain, duration; exam.
  • Cavernosal blood gas and Doppler ultrasound to distinguish low vs high flow; CTA/angiography selectively.

Planning & technique

  • Superselect internal pudendal/cavernosal artery and embolise fistula using temporary or permanent agent tailored to anatomy and recurrence risk.
  • Preserve erectile tissue perfusion.

Immediate post-care

  • Observe detumescence, pain and access site; urology review.

Follow-up

  • Assess recurrence and erectile function.

Complications & rescue

  • Erectile dysfunction, recurrence/recanalisation, non-target penile ischaemia.

EBIR pearls

  • First distinguish high-flow from low-flow. The latter is a time-critical ischaemic emergency.
Iliofemoral DVT thrombectomy / venous stentingB · Vascular · 2.2.1.3.1 · HIGH-YIELD›
Selected thrombus-removal and venous reconstruction for severe acute iliofemoral DVT or chronic iliocaval obstruction.

Indications

  • Selected acute iliofemoral DVT with severe symptoms, low bleeding risk and good functional status where thrombus removal is expected to add benefit.
  • Phlegmasia / threatened limb.
  • Symptomatic chronic iliocaval obstruction with significant venous hypertension.

Contraindications / cautions

  • High bleeding risk when lysis is planned; limited life expectancy/functional benefit; asymptomatic disease.
  • Caution: renal failure, active infection, inability to maintain anticoagulation.

Work-up

  • Duplex + CT/MR venography as needed; determine acute vs chronic extent and IVC involvement.
  • FBC/coagulation/renal function; anticoagulation history and bleeding risk.
  • Assess for underlying compressive lesion and malignancy when appropriate.

Planning & technique

  • Venous access via popliteal/femoral/jugular route according to anatomy.
  • Aspiration/mechanical thrombectomy ± limited lysis; use IVUS where available to define iliac obstruction and landing zones.
  • Stent fixed iliocaval obstruction with adequate inflow/outflow; avoid leaving untreated residual disease.

Immediate post-care

  • Therapeutic anticoagulation per thrombosis/venous stent protocol; compression and mobilisation when appropriate.

Follow-up

  • Clinical review and duplex/venous imaging for patency; investigate recurrent swelling.

Complications & rescue

  • Bleeding/PE, venous rupture, stent thrombosis/migration, contralateral iliac compromise.

EBIR pearls

  • Do not stent acute thrombus; clear thrombus, define the fixed lesion, then stent.
  • Adequate inflow is crucial for venous stent patency.
Pulmonary embolism catheter therapyB · Vascular · 2.2.1.3.2 · HIGH-YIELD · ON-CALL›
Risk-stratified catheter-directed therapy for acute pulmonary embolism as part of a multidisciplinary PE response pathway.

Indications

  • High-risk PE with haemodynamic instability when systemic thrombolysis is contraindicated/failed or a catheter strategy is chosen by the treating team.
  • Selected intermediate-high-risk PE with deterioration or high-risk features after multidisciplinary assessment.

Contraindications / cautions

  • No meaningful thrombus target or patient whose instability is not primarily due to PE.
  • Thrombolysis contraindications matter if catheter-directed lytic is used; mechanical thrombectomy may avoid lytic.

Work-up

  • Confirm CTPA clot burden and RV strain; clinical risk stratification, oxygen/haemodynamics.
  • ECG/troponin/BNP and echo where useful; FBC/coagulation/renal function; therapeutic anticoagulation unless contraindicated.

Planning & technique

  • Femoral/jugular venous access; pulmonary angiography selectively.
  • Use aspiration/mechanical thrombectomy and/or local lysis according to anatomy, physiology and device availability.
  • Endpoint is haemodynamic/clinical improvement with safe thrombus reduction—not angiographic perfection.

Immediate post-care

  • Monitored/ICU setting; continue anticoagulation; observe access, Hb, oxygenation and RV status.

Follow-up

  • Define duration/cause of anticoagulation; evaluate persistent dyspnoea for chronic thromboembolic disease.

Complications & rescue

  • Pulmonary artery injury/haemoptysis, bleeding, arrhythmia, haemodynamic collapse, access complications.

EBIR pearls

  • PE treatment is risk-stratified; “large clot on CT” alone is not an indication.
  • In unstable PE, the endpoint is restoring physiology quickly.
IVC filter placement / retrievalB · Vascular · 2.2.1.3.2›
Temporary mechanical PE protection in narrow indications, with an explicit retrieval plan at the time of insertion.

Indications

  • Acute VTE with an absolute contraindication to therapeutic anticoagulation or major complication of anticoagulation; selected other indications are highly context dependent.

Contraindications / cautions

  • No valid indication or inability to access a suitable cava.
  • Caution: caval thrombosis, congenital variants, pregnancy, very large cava depending on device.

Work-up

  • Confirm VTE and reason anticoagulation cannot be used; document intended duration.
  • Review CT for caval anatomy/diameter, renal veins, duplication or thrombus; coagulation/FBC as appropriate.

Planning & technique

  • US-guided jugular/femoral access; cavography when needed; deploy below renal veins unless specific indication requires otherwise.
  • At retrieval: assess tilt, penetration, thrombus and dwell-time complexity; use advanced techniques if required.

Immediate post-care

  • Restart anticoagulation when safe; create a trackable retrieval referral before discharge.

Follow-up

  • Actively retrieve when PE risk/contraindication resolves; do not rely on passive recall.

Complications & rescue

  • Caval thrombosis, migration, tilt, penetration, fracture/embolisation, difficult retrieval.

EBIR pearls

  • The best filter is often the one that is retrieved promptly.
  • “Contraindication to anticoagulation” is the classic EBIR indication.
TIPSB · Vascular · 2.2.1.3.4.1 · HIGH-YIELD›
Portosystemic decompression for selected portal-hypertension complications with careful liver, cardiac and encephalopathy assessment.

Indications

  • Acute variceal bleeding in selected high-risk/failed endoscopic cases and rescue situations.
  • Refractory/recurrent ascites or hydrothorax in appropriately selected patients.
  • Selected portal hypertensive complications.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Severe right-heart failure or severe pulmonary hypertension, uncontrolled systemic infection, severe liver failure where TIPS would be futile/harmful.
  • Relative: recurrent/refractory encephalopathy, biliary obstruction, extensive hepatic malignancy or anatomy that makes safe creation difficult.

Work-up

  • Clinical: indication, encephalopathy, ascites, transplant candidacy, infection.
  • Labs: FBC, INR, LFT/albumin/bilirubin, renal function/electrolytes; calculate MELD-type risk assessment.
  • Doppler/CT/MRI portal/hepatic vein anatomy and patency; echocardiographic cardiac assessment.

Planning & technique

  • Right IJ access → hepatic vein → portal puncture; portal venography and pressure measurements.
  • Deploy covered stent-graft, dilate to achieve adequate decompression while balancing encephalopathy risk.
  • Recheck portosystemic gradient and exclude complications.

Immediate post-care

  • Monitor bleeding, haemodynamics, liver/renal function and encephalopathy.
  • Doppler baseline according to institutional protocol; adjust diuretics/encephalopathy therapy with hepatology.

Follow-up

  • Clinical response + Doppler surveillance; investigate recurrent portal-hypertension symptoms for stenosis/thrombosis.

Complications & rescue

  • Hepatic encephalopathy, liver failure, heart failure, haemoperitoneum/capsular puncture, biliary fistula, haemolysis, stent dysfunction.

EBIR pearls

  • Pre-TIPS cardiac assessment is not optional.
  • State the pressure-gradient concept and balance decompression against encephalopathy.
BRTO / retrograde variceal obliterationB · Vascular · 2.2.1.3.4.1›
Obliteration of gastric varices through a spontaneous portosystemic shunt, often complementary to rather than interchangeable with TIPS.

Indications

  • Gastric variceal bleeding or high-risk gastric varices with a suitable gastrorenal/splenorenal shunt.
  • Selected hepatic encephalopathy driven by a large spontaneous portosystemic shunt.

Contraindications / cautions

  • No accessible shunt anatomy.
  • Portal/mesenteric venous thrombosis or severe portal hypertension may make shunt occlusion hazardous; assess individually.

Work-up

  • Portal venous CT/MRI: varices, shunt anatomy, portal patency, ascites.
  • Liver/renal function, FBC/coagulation; endoscopic/hepatology context.

Planning & technique

  • Retrograde shunt catheterisation; occlude outflow and embolise/sclerose variceal complex using local BRTO/PARTO/CARTO technique.
  • Confirm no dangerous systemic escape pathways.

Immediate post-care

  • Monitor haemoglobin, haemolysis/renal effects where sclerosants used, portal-hypertension symptoms.

Follow-up

  • Endoscopic/imaging follow-up; watch for worsening oesophageal varices/ascites due to increased portal pressure.

Complications & rescue

  • Haemoglobinuria/renal injury with some sclerosants, PE/non-target embolisation, worsening ascites/oesophageal varices, portal thrombosis.

EBIR pearls

  • TIPS decreases portal pressure; BRTO occludes a decompressive shunt and may increase it.
Budd–Chiari interventionB · Vascular · 2.2.1.3.4.2›
Step-up endovascular management of hepatic venous outflow obstruction, from angioplasty/stenting to TIPS.

Indications

  • Symptomatic hepatic vein/IVC obstruction with a treatable short stenosis/occlusion; TIPS for diffuse hepatic venous occlusion or failure of lesser therapy.

Contraindications / cautions

  • End-stage liver failure requiring transplant rather than endovascular salvage; uncontrolled infection.

Work-up

  • Doppler + CT/MRI to define hepatic vein/IVC obstruction, collaterals and portal patency.
  • Liver/renal function, coagulation/FBC; thrombophilia/myeloproliferative work-up with hepatology/haematology.

Planning & technique

  • Venography/pressure measurements; recanalise and angioplasty ± stent focal obstruction.
  • TIPS/DIPS may be needed for diffuse disease.

Immediate post-care

  • Anticoagulation when appropriate; monitor liver function, bleeding and stent patency.

Follow-up

  • Doppler surveillance and underlying prothrombotic disease treatment.

Complications & rescue

  • Bleeding, capsular perforation, stent thrombosis, liver failure, encephalopathy after TIPS.

EBIR pearls

  • Think step-up: anticoagulation → recanalisation/angioplasty → TIPS → transplant depending on anatomy and response.
Dialysis fistula/graft interventionB · Vascular · 2.2.1.3.6 · HIGH-YIELD›
Salvage of dysfunctional haemodialysis access by treating the haemodynamically significant lesion and preserving future access options.

Indications

  • Clinical dialysis dysfunction with corresponding stenosis: poor flows, high venous pressures, difficult cannulation, prolonged bleeding, recirculation or thrombosis.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Infected graft/access usually requires surgical/infectious management.
  • No clinical dysfunction plus incidental stenosis generally does not justify routine angioplasty.

Work-up

  • Examine access: pulse/thrill, aneurysm, infection, collateral veins, arm swelling.
  • Review dialysis flow/pressure trends; duplex when useful; potassium and coagulation as clinically indicated.

Planning & technique

  • Fistulography from arterial inflow through central veins as indicated.
  • Angioplasty causative stenosis; high-pressure/cutting/scoring or stent-graft for selected resistant/recurrent lesions.
  • For thrombosis: thrombectomy + correct underlying stenosis before declaring success.

Immediate post-care

  • Check thrill, haemostasis, distal hand perfusion and ability to dialyse.

Follow-up

  • Dialysis-unit clinical monitoring; repeat imaging/intervention based on recurrent dysfunction, not scheduled angiography alone.

Complications & rescue

  • Venous rupture → balloon tamponade ± stent-graft.
  • Arterial embolisation, access thrombosis, steal/hand ischaemia, haematoma.

EBIR pearls

  • A thrombosed access nearly always has a causative stenosis—thrombus removal alone is incomplete treatment.
Central venous access / SVC stentingB · Vascular · 2.2.1.3.7›
Safe image-guided venous access and treatment of symptomatic central venous obstruction.

Indications

  • Reliable venous access for therapy/dialysis when clinically indicated.
  • SVC/central venous obstruction causing significant symptoms, often malignant or device-related.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Active infection at proposed tunnel/pocket; no suitable venous route; uncorrectable coagulation issue depending on procedure.

Work-up

  • Confirm indication, expected duration and infusion/device requirements.
  • US venous patency; review prior devices and CT for central obstruction when relevant.
  • FBC/coagulation and infection status as appropriate.

Planning & technique

  • Real-time US puncture; fluoroscopic tip position; sterile tunnel/pocket technique.
  • For SVC obstruction: venography ± CT road map; angioplasty/stent across symptomatic obstruction while preserving key inflow.

Immediate post-care

  • Chest imaging only when clinically/technically indicated by access route/local protocol.
  • Line function, pocket/tunnel and arm/neck swelling review.

Follow-up

  • Line care and prompt assessment for infection/thrombosis; stent re-evaluation if symptoms recur.

Complications & rescue

  • Pneumothorax/haemothorax, arterial puncture, malposition, infection, thrombosis, pinch-off/fracture, air embolism.
  • SVC stent migration/rupture and cardiac tamponade are rare but severe.

EBIR pearls

  • Ultrasound-guided IJ access avoids many historic line complications.
  • For SVC syndrome, treat symptoms and anatomy; not every radiologic stenosis needs a stent.
Adrenal venous samplingB · Vascular · 2.2.1.3.8›
Functional lateralisation test for primary aldosteronism requiring protocol discipline and technically successful bilateral sampling.

Indications

  • Biochemically confirmed primary aldosteronism when lateralisation will alter management and imaging alone is insufficient.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Patient not a surgical candidate or result will not change management.

Work-up

  • Endocrine confirmation and medication/potassium optimisation per endocrine protocol.
  • Review adrenal CT for masses and right adrenal vein anatomy.
  • Know whether cosyntropin-stimulated or unstimulated protocol is used and exact timed sample requirements.

Planning & technique

  • Right adrenal vein is the technical challenge; use careful venography with minimal injection.
  • Sample bilateral adrenal veins and peripheral vein according to protocol; label immediately and unambiguously.

Immediate post-care

  • Short access-site observation.

Follow-up

  • Interpret with endocrinology using selectivity and lateralisation indices appropriate to the local protocol.

Complications & rescue

  • Adrenal vein rupture/haemorrhage, thrombosis, access complications; failed/nonselective sampling.

EBIR pearls

  • A beautifully placed catheter is irrelevant if the sample is mislabeled or timing is wrong.

2.2.2 Chest, GI & hepatobiliary

Image-guided percutaneous biopsyC · Non-vascular · 2.2.2.1 · HIGH-YIELD›
Tissue acquisition planned around diagnostic yield, safe trajectory, pathology requirements and management of haemorrhage/pneumothorax.

Indications

  • Tissue diagnosis will change staging, treatment or management and there is a safely accessible target.

Contraindications / cautions

  • No management consequence, inaccessible target, or uncorrectable bleeding risk for the chosen organ/route.
  • Caution: severe pulmonary hypertension/poor respiratory reserve for lung biopsy, highly vascular lesions, suspected pheochromocytoma without preparation.

Work-up

  • Review all imaging and choose viable target, safest route and modality.
  • Clarify pathology requirements: histology, cytology, microbiology, molecular testing, lymphoma flow cytometry etc.
  • FBC/coagulation according to bleeding risk; antithrombotic plan; consent organ-specific risks.

Planning & technique

  • Use coaxial technique for repeated cores where appropriate.
  • Avoid pleura, bowel, major vessels and tumour seeding pathways; for suspected sarcoma coordinate biopsy tract with surgical team.
  • Confirm specimen adequacy/handling before ending the case when possible.

Immediate post-care

  • Organ-specific monitoring: chest imaging/US for lung, haemoglobin/pain for solid organs.
  • Provide escalation instructions for dyspnoea, bleeding, fever or severe pain.

Follow-up

  • Track pathology result and adequacy; repeat biopsy if non-diagnostic only after reviewing why it failed.

Complications & rescue

  • Bleeding/haematoma/haemoperitoneum, pneumothorax/air embolism (lung), infection, adjacent-organ injury, tumour seeding (rare).

EBIR pearls

  • The diagnostic question determines the specimen—not the needle you happen to have.
  • For sarcoma, biopsy trajectory is part of the future resection.
Abscess / collection drainageC · Non-vascular · 2.2.2.3 · HIGH-YIELD · ON-CALL›
Source-control procedure requiring a safe access route, appropriate catheter strategy, microbiology and active drain follow-up.

Indications

  • Drainable infected or symptomatic collection where percutaneous source control is appropriate.
  • Diagnostic aspiration when the nature of a collection will change management.

Contraindications / cautions

  • No safe route, non-drainable phlegmon, or collection unlikely to benefit from drainage.
  • Coagulopathy and sepsis are optimised in parallel according to urgency.

Work-up

  • Review imaging for size, loculation, contents and bowel/pleural/vascular relationships.
  • FBC/CRP, coagulation, cultures/antibiotics; ensure specimen plan.
  • Choose US/CT/fluoro guidance and transorgan route only when justified.

Planning & technique

  • Seldinger or trocar depending on target; place sideholes fully within collection.
  • Send aspirate promptly for microbiology; consider multiple drains, larger calibre, flushing or fibrinolytic strategy for complex cavities.

Immediate post-care

  • Record output and character; prescribe flush regimen and securement.
  • Monitor sepsis response, bleeding and drain-related pain.

Follow-up

  • Clinical/lab response + drain output; CT/US if non-resolving.
  • Remove when source controlled and output/imaging criteria are met; check for fistula when output persists.

Complications & rescue

  • Bleeding, bowel/solid-organ injury, pleural transgression/empyema, septic shock, drain dislodgement/occlusion, fistula.

EBIR pearls

  • Drain management after insertion is part of the procedure.
  • Persistent output may be telling you there is a fistula, not that the catheter is “working well”.
Pleural drain / chest tubeC · Non-vascular · 2.2.2.3 · ON-CALL›
Image-guided pleural drainage with attention to indication, safe triangle/trajectory, drainage system and persistent air leak.

Indications

  • Symptomatic or clinically significant pleural fluid, empyema/complicated parapneumonic effusion, pneumothorax requiring drainage.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Very small/asymptomatic collection not requiring drainage; no safe window.
  • Coagulopathy considered according to urgency and route.

Work-up

  • US immediately before fluid drainage; CT for loculated/complex collections.
  • Review FBC/coagulation and respiratory status; decide fluid tests.

Planning & technique

  • Enter above rib to avoid neurovascular bundle; ultrasound guidance for fluid.
  • Place sideholes within pleural space and connect to appropriate water-seal/suction system.

Immediate post-care

  • Monitor respiratory status, drain swing/bubbling/output and pain; chest imaging as clinically required.

Follow-up

  • Evaluate persistent air leak, loculations and lung re-expansion; remove according to clinical/output/air-leak criteria.

Complications & rescue

  • Intercostal vessel injury/haemothorax, lung injury, infection, re-expansion pulmonary oedema, subcutaneous drain placement.

EBIR pearls

  • A drain is a system: catheter + sideholes + connections + bottle + patient position.
Lymphangiography / lymphatic embolisationC · Non-vascular · 2.2.2.2›
Intranodal lymphangiography and targeted embolisation for postoperative or spontaneous lymphatic leaks.

Indications

  • Persistent high-output chylothorax/chylous ascites or localised postoperative lymphatic leak not resolving with conservative measures.

Contraindications / cautions

  • No clear clinical indication; caution in severe respiratory compromise where oil-based contrast load may be poorly tolerated.

Work-up

  • Confirm chylous nature where relevant (triglycerides/chylomicrons); review operative history and cross-sectional imaging.
  • Plan intranodal access and likely leak level.

Planning & technique

  • Bilateral inguinal intranodal lymphangiography using controlled oil-based contrast injection.
  • Identify leak; thoracic duct/cisterna chyli access via transabdominal or retrograde route when needed; embolise leak/outflow.

Immediate post-care

  • Monitor respiratory status and embolisation-related symptoms; continue dietary/medical therapy until output falls.

Follow-up

  • Drain-output trend and imaging as needed; repeat intervention for persistent leak.

Complications & rescue

  • Oil embolisation, infection, access bleeding, non-target embolisation, rare neurologic/pulmonary complications.

EBIR pearls

  • Diagnostic lymphangiography alone can be therapeutic in some leaks.
Percutaneous gastrostomy / gastrojejunostomyC · Non-vascular · 2.2.2.4.1 · HIGH-YIELD›
Image-guided enteral access with pre-procedure assessment of anatomy, aspiration risk, gastropexy and tube-care pathway.

Indications

  • Long-term enteral nutrition or decompression when oral intake is inadequate and stomach/jejunum is accessible.

Contraindications / cautions

  • No safe gastric access, uncontrolled peritonitis, severe uncorrectable coagulopathy.
  • Relative: massive ascites, interposed colon/liver, gastric varices, severe gastroparesis/aspiration risk may alter approach.

Work-up

  • Review CT/US for stomach, liver and colon position; consider NG insufflation.
  • FBC/coagulation; antibiotics per local protocol; nutrition/SLT input and goals of care.

Planning & technique

  • Insufflate stomach, select safe puncture, gastropexy where used, serial dilatation and tube placement under fluoroscopy.
  • For GJ, ensure jejunal tip beyond ligament of Treitz when feasible and avoid excessive intragastric loop.

Immediate post-care

  • Follow local fasting/commencement-of-feeds protocol; inspect site and educate on tube care.

Follow-up

  • Tube exchange/maintenance pathway; review leakage, granulation, blockage and dislodgement.

Complications & rescue

  • Peritonitis from intraperitoneal placement/leak, bleeding, aspiration, infection, buried/dislodged tube, transcolonic placement.

EBIR pearls

  • Interposed colon is a preventable catastrophe—prove your access route.
Oesophageal / gastroduodenal stentingC · Non-vascular · 2.2.2.4.2›
Palliation or selected bridge therapy for malignant GI obstruction using lesion-length, migration and perforation-aware planning.

Indications

  • Malignant dysphagia or gastroduodenal obstruction where rapid luminal palliation is appropriate.
  • Selected benign indications only after multidisciplinary consideration.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Perforation/peritonitis, inability to cross lesion safely, obstruction better served by surgery/endoscopy.

Work-up

  • Endoscopy/CT to define level, length, fistula, adjacent anatomy and tumour extent.
  • Assess aspiration risk, nutrition and goals of care.

Planning & technique

  • Cross lesion gently with wire/catheter; define proximal/distal margins.
  • Choose covered vs uncovered stent based on tumour ingrowth, migration and fistula considerations; ensure adequate overlap.

Immediate post-care

  • Diet progression and aspiration precautions; manage pain/reflux.

Follow-up

  • Clinical review for recurrent dysphagia/obstruction; evaluate migration, food bolus or tumour ingrowth/overgrowth.

Complications & rescue

  • Perforation, bleeding, migration, reflux, fistula, airway compression, re-obstruction.

EBIR pearls

  • Stent choice is a trade-off: covered resists ingrowth but migrates more.
PTC / biliary drainage / stentingC · Non-vascular · 2.2.2.5 · HIGH-YIELD · ON-CALL›
Percutaneous decompression of malignant/benign biliary obstruction with sepsis control, segmental planning and staged internalisation when appropriate.

Indications

  • Obstructed biliary system requiring decompression when endoscopic drainage is failed, unavailable or inappropriate.
  • Cholangitis requiring urgent source control; malignant palliation or bridge to surgery.

Contraindications / cautions

  • No absolute contraindication in life-threatening cholangitis; coagulopathy/ascites increase risk and should be optimised where possible.

Work-up

  • Review contrast imaging: level of obstruction, ducts that need drainage, lobar atrophy, portal patency and planned surgical resection.
  • FBC, INR/platelets, LFT/bilirubin, renal function, sepsis markers/blood cultures; antibiotic coverage.
  • Decide external vs internal-external vs primary stenting and which segments actually require drainage.

Planning & technique

  • US-guided peripheral duct puncture; avoid central hilar puncture where possible.
  • Cross obstruction when safe; cholangiography should be limited in infected obstruction to avoid pressurising undrained ducts.
  • Secure drain with sideholes spanning intended segments; staged metallic stenting for malignant obstruction when indicated.

Immediate post-care

  • Monitor sepsis, bile output, fluid/electrolyte loss, haemobilia and drain position.
  • Flush/cap only according to documented protocol and clinical condition.

Follow-up

  • Trend bilirubin/LFTs and symptoms; cholangiogram/exchange/stent pathway.
  • Persistent fever after unilateral hilar drainage may mean an undrained infected segment.

Complications & rescue

  • Sepsis/cholangitis, haemobilia/arterial injury, bile leak/peritonitis, pleural transgression, drain dislodgement, pancreatitis.

EBIR pearls

  • In hilar obstruction, “drain the liver” is too simplistic—drain functional segments and avoid contaminating undrained ducts.
Percutaneous cholecystostomyC · Non-vascular · 2.2.2.5 · ON-CALL›
Gallbladder decompression for selected acute cholecystitis patients who are poor immediate surgical candidates.

Indications

  • Acute cholecystitis with sepsis/organ dysfunction or failure of conservative therapy when urgent cholecystectomy is not suitable.

  • Cholangiogram and tube-removal strategy after clinical resolution; plan definitive cholecystectomy or long-term drain strategy with surgery.

  • The procedure is a bridge or destination therapy only after you define what happens to the gallbladder next.

  • Haemorrhage, fracture, infection, neurovascular injury, tumour contamination of inappropriate biopsy tract.

EBIR pearls

  • The safest geometric route is not necessarily the oncologically correct route—coordinate with the surgeon.
Vertebroplasty / cementoplastyE · MSK · 2.2.4.4–5›
Percutaneous cement augmentation for selected painful structural lesions with careful clinical-radiologic correlation and cement-leak prevention.

Indications

  • Selected painful vertebral compression fractures refractory to appropriate conservative care.
  • Painful osteolytic lesions requiring structural augmentation, often combined with ablation.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Asymptomatic fracture, uncontrolled infection, uncorrectable coagulopathy.
  • Relative: unstable burst fracture with significant retropulsion/neurologic compromise, severe canal compromise.

Work-up

  • MRI/CT to confirm symptomatic level, acuity, posterior wall and neural anatomy.
  • Clinical localisation of pain; FBC/coagulation and infection assessment.

Planning & technique

  • Transpedicular/parapedicular access as appropriate; high-viscosity cement under continuous imaging.
  • Stop for epidural/venous/discal extraosseous leak or adequate fill—do not chase complete vertebral opacification.

Immediate post-care

  • Neurological exam and pain assessment; mobilise according to local protocol.

Follow-up

  • Clinical response; image for new neurological symptoms or recurrent pain.

Complications & rescue

  • Cement leak causing radiculopathy/spinal cord compression, pulmonary cement emboli, infection, bleeding, adjacent fracture.

EBIR pearls

  • Treat the patient’s pain generator, not an MRI finding.
Spinal injection / nerve root / facet interventionE · MSK · 2.2.4.5.2›
Targeted diagnostic or therapeutic spinal injection with strict attention to level, needle position and drug safety.

Indications

  • Selected radicular or facet-mediated pain where image-guided injection is clinically appropriate.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Local/systemic infection, uncorrectable coagulopathy, no clinical-radiologic target.
  • Caution with particulate steroid in high-risk arterial territories according to local safety practice.

Work-up

  • Clinical pain pattern + MRI/CT correlation; antithrombotic review and neurological baseline.

Planning & technique

  • Use image guidance and contrast confirmation where appropriate before therapeutic injectate.
  • Avoid intravascular/intrathecal injection; use small test injection and local spinal safety protocol.

Immediate post-care

  • Short observation; document neuro status and post-injection warning symptoms.

Follow-up

  • Assess diagnostic and therapeutic response; avoid repetitive procedures without benefit.

Complications & rescue

  • Epidural haematoma/abscess, nerve injury, inadvertent intrathecal or arterial injection, steroid effects.

EBIR pearls

  • A diagnostic block is only useful if the pre- and post-block clinical question is defined.

2.2.5 Interventional oncology

TACEF · Interventional oncology · 2.2.5.2 · HIGH-YIELD›
Arterial locoregional therapy balancing tumour control with preservation of non-tumour liver and integration with systemic/surgical options.

Indications

  • Commonly used for selected unresectable liver-dominant tumours, especially HCC according to stage, liver reserve and MDT strategy.
  • Can be used in selected metastatic disease depending on tumour biology and treatment pathway.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Decompensated liver failure, severe portal-flow compromise without a safe strategy, uncontrolled infection, extensive tumour burden where benefit is unlikely.
  • Relative: severe renal dysfunction, biliary-enteric anastomosis/high infection risk, poor performance status.

Work-up

  • MDT indication; multiphase CT/MRI, tumour distribution and arterial anatomy.
  • Liver reserve: bilirubin/albumin/INR, ascites/encephalopathy, renal function, FBC; performance status.
  • Review portal vein patency and prior biliary intervention; prophylactic antibiotic strategy in high-risk biliary colonisation.

Planning & technique

  • Selective/superselective catheterisation whenever feasible.
  • Deliver conventional or drug-eluting chemoembolisation strategy according to tumour and local protocol; avoid reflux/non-target flow.
  • Endpoint is adequate tumour arterial devascularisation/near stasis without excessive normal-liver embolisation.

Immediate post-care

  • Pain/antiemetic care; monitor post-embolisation syndrome, LFTs, fever and hepatic decompensation.

Follow-up

  • Cross-sectional response assessment using enhancement-based criteria and liver function, commonly around 4–8 weeks depending on pathway.
  • Retreat only if residual viable tumour and liver reserve/overall strategy support it.

Complications & rescue

  • Post-embolisation syndrome, liver failure, abscess (higher with biliary instrumentation), cholecystitis, pancreatitis, non-target GI injury, arterial damage.

EBIR pearls

  • Tumour response is about viable enhancement, not just size.
  • Superselective treatment preserves future liver options.
Y-90 radioembolisation / TAREF · Interventional oncology · 2.2.5.2 · HIGH-YIELD›
Internal radiotherapy requiring mapping, extrahepatic protection, dosimetry and liver-reserve assessment.

Indications

  • Selected HCC, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma or liver-dominant metastases in an MDT-defined setting; radiation segmentectomy/lobectomy concepts may be used.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Uncorrectable significant extrahepatic shunting/deposition or lung shunt beyond safe device/dosimetry limits.
  • Severely impaired liver reserve or unsuitable vascular anatomy.

Work-up

  • Multiphase CT/MRI, tumour/treated-liver volumes, liver function/performance status.
  • Mapping angiography with identification/protection of extrahepatic branches and Tc-99m MAA (or validated local mapping method) for distribution/shunt assessment.
  • Patient-specific dosimetry planning; review prior systemic/radiation therapy.

Planning & technique

  • Reproduce mapping catheter position; meticulous avoidance of non-target flow.
  • Administer planned activity according to dosimetry/device protocol; measure residual activity and document delivered dose.

Immediate post-care

  • Radiation safety instructions; monitor fatigue, pain, nausea and liver function.

Follow-up

  • Imaging + labs at defined interval; expect delayed radiographic response and post-radiation changes.

Complications & rescue

  • Radioembolisation-induced liver disease, radiation gastritis/ulcer, cholecystitis, pneumonitis, biliary injury, non-target deposition.

EBIR pearls

  • Mapping is part of the treatment, not a preliminary angiogram.
  • Know the difference between activity (GBq) and absorbed dose (Gy).
Bland hepatic embolisationF · Interventional oncology · 2.2.5.2›
Tumour ischaemia using embolic particles without chemotherapy, often used for hypervascular liver metastases such as neuroendocrine tumours.

Indications

  • Selected hypervascular liver-dominant tumours when arterial embolisation is appropriate within the oncologic strategy.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Poor liver reserve, unsafe arterial anatomy, uncontrolled infection; portal compromise increases risk.

Work-up

  • Same core liver reserve/tumour distribution work-up as TACE; understand tumour biology and alternative systemic/locoregional options.

Planning & technique

  • Selective tumour-bearing arterial embolisation to controlled stasis; preserve non-tumour liver.

Immediate post-care

  • Post-embolisation syndrome and liver function monitoring.

Follow-up

  • Enhancement-based response and clinical symptom response (e.g. functional NET syndrome where relevant).

Complications & rescue

  • Liver failure, abscess, non-target ischaemia, cholecystitis, post-embolisation syndrome.

EBIR pearls

  • “Bland” describes the embolic, not a low-risk procedure.
Percutaneous liver tumour ablationF · Interventional oncology · 2.2.5.3.1 · HIGH-YIELD›
Image-guided thermal ablation with margin-based planning and protection of bile ducts, bowel, diaphragm and other vulnerable structures.

Indications

  • Selected primary or metastatic liver tumours where local ablation offers curative, consolidative or palliative benefit within MDT strategy.

Contraindications / cautions

  • No safe ablation margin due to critical structures without a protective technique; decompensated liver failure; uncontrolled infection.
  • Caution: central bile ducts, bowel, gallbladder, diaphragm, large-vessel heat-sink depending on modality.

Work-up

  • Contrast MRI/CT to define lesion number/size/location and prior therapy; staging imaging.
  • Liver/renal function, FBC/coagulation, tumour markers as relevant; anaesthetic assessment.
  • Plan probe trajectory, ablation geometry and target margin; consider hydro/pneumodissection, biliary cooling or artificial ascites.

Planning & technique

  • Place probe(s) to cover tumour + intended circumferential margin.
  • Use CT/US/fusion/CBCT as appropriate; monitor vulnerable structures and reposition/protect if required.
  • Immediate contrast assessment when feasible to detect residual enhancement and complications.

Immediate post-care

  • Pain, bleeding, fever, LFTs; watch for diaphragmatic/pleural injury and biliary complications.

Follow-up

  • Contrast CT/MRI at institutional interval (often ~4–6 weeks) for complete non-enhancing ablation zone and adequate margin; then oncologic surveillance.

Complications & rescue

  • Bleeding, abscess, biliary stricture/biloma, bowel perforation, diaphragmatic injury, pleural effusion, liver failure, tumour seeding (rare).

EBIR pearls

  • Ablation success is a 3-D margin problem, not “the probe went through the lesion”.
Renal tumour ablationF · Interventional oncology · 2.2.5.3.1 · HIGH-YIELD›
Nephron-sparing local treatment for selected renal tumours using hydrodissection and collecting-system/nerve protection.

Indications

  • Selected small renal masses, particularly in patients favouring nephron preservation or at higher surgical risk; hereditary/multiple tumours may particularly benefit.

Contraindications / cautions

  • No safe percutaneous window or tumour anatomy where surgery/active surveillance is clearly preferred.
  • Caution: central/hilar lesions, collecting system/ureter, bowel, genitofemoral nerve.

Work-up

  • Renal protocol CT/MRI and staging; renal function/FBC/coagulation.
  • Confirm histology strategy—biopsy before or at ablation according to local oncologic pathway.
  • Plan patient position and displacement/protection of bowel and ureter.

Planning & technique

  • Cryo offers visible ice ball; MWA/RFA are alternatives depending on lesion/location and expertise.
  • Hydrodissection/pneumodissection; pyeloperfusion or ureteric protection for collecting-system risk when used locally.
  • Cover tumour with adequate margin.

Immediate post-care

  • Monitor haematuria, pain, Hb and renal function.

Follow-up

  • Contrast CT/MRI for residual enhancement, then long-term renal oncologic surveillance.

Complications & rescue

  • Perirenal haematoma/haemorrhage, urinary leak/stricture, bowel injury, neuropathy, infection, renal function loss.

EBIR pearls

  • For anterior renal tumours, bowel displacement is often the key technical step.
Lung tumour ablationF · Interventional oncology · 2.2.5.3.1 · HIGH-YIELD›
Percutaneous ablation of selected primary or metastatic lung tumours with pleural, airway and pulmonary-reserve planning.

Indications

  • Selected early primary lung cancer in non-surgical candidates and oligometastatic/oligoprogressive lung disease within MDT strategy.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Insufficient pulmonary reserve, uncontrolled infection, uncorrectable coagulopathy; central/hilar lesions may carry unacceptable airway/vessel risk.

Work-up

  • Thin-slice CT/PET-CT staging as appropriate; pulmonary function and anaesthetic risk.
  • FBC/coagulation; review anticoagulation and target relation to pleura, fissures, vessels, bronchi, diaphragm.

Planning & technique

  • Plan shortest safe route avoiding fissures/bullae when possible.
  • Ablate tumour with adequate margin; modality choice depends on size/location and expertise.
  • Immediate CT to assess pneumothorax/haemorrhage and technical coverage.

Immediate post-care

  • Serial respiratory observations; treat pneumothorax based on size/symptoms and progression; analgesia.

Follow-up

  • CT/PET pattern evolves after ablation; assess for marginal nodular enhancement/growth rather than expecting immediate size reduction.

Complications & rescue

  • Pneumothorax, pulmonary haemorrhage/haemoptysis, pleural effusion, bronchopleural fistula, infection, rare air embolism.

EBIR pearls

  • Post-ablation inflammation can look bigger before it looks better.
Bone tumour ablation ± cement / fixationF · Interventional oncology · 2.2.5.3.4›
Percutaneous pain palliation or local control combining ablation with structural augmentation when fracture risk requires it.

Indications

  • Painful bone metastasis refractory to standard therapy, oligometastatic local control, selected benign painful tumours.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Impending/pathologic fracture requiring surgical stabilisation rather than percutaneous treatment alone; uncontrolled infection.
  • Caution: lesions near spinal cord, major nerves, articular cartilage or growth plate.

Work-up

  • CT/MRI ± PET; assess fracture risk, mechanical instability and neural proximity; coordinate oncology/radiotherapy/orthopaedics.
  • FBC/coagulation and analgesia/anaesthesia plan.

Planning & technique

  • Choose modality based on lesion and critical structures; cryo allows visible ice-ball monitoring.
  • Use thermocouples, hydrodissection, CO₂ or other protection as appropriate.
  • Add cementoplasty/screw fixation when mechanical support is required.

Immediate post-care

  • Neurovascular exam, pain management and weight-bearing plan.

Follow-up

  • Pain score/function and imaging for local control/structural integrity.

Complications & rescue

  • Nerve injury, skin necrosis, fracture, cement leak, bleeding, infection, thermal injury to joint/cord.

EBIR pearls

  • Tumour kill and mechanical stability are separate endpoints—address both.
Portal vein embolisation / liver venous deprivationF · Interventional oncology · 2.2.5.2 · HIGH-YIELD›
Preoperative liver regeneration therapy based on future liver remnant volume/function and planned surgical resection.

Indications

  • Planned major hepatectomy with insufficient predicted future liver remnant volume/function for the patient’s liver quality.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Unresectable/progressive disease where hypertrophy will not lead to surgery; portal vein thrombosis/anatomy preventing safe intended strategy.

Work-up

  • High-quality portal/hepatic venous CT/MRI and surgical plan.
  • Calculate FLR volume indexed to patient/liver; functional assessment when appropriate (e.g. hepatobiliary scintigraphy).
  • Liver function/FBC/coagulation and tumour progression risk.

Planning & technique

  • Embolise portal branches feeding the liver to be resected while preserving FLR portal inflow.
  • LVD/DVE adds hepatic venous embolisation in selected pathways to increase hypertrophy.
  • Avoid non-target FLR embolisation and portal thrombosis.

Immediate post-care

  • Pain/fever and liver function; monitor for portal thrombosis or liver decompensation.

Follow-up

  • Repeat volumetric ± functional assessment typically after a defined regeneration interval; reassess resectability and interval tumour progression.

Complications & rescue

  • Non-target portal embolisation, portal vein thrombosis, haemorrhage, infection, liver failure, tumour progression during interval.

EBIR pearls

  • The target is an adequate functional FLR, not a percentage hypertrophy for its own sake.
Tumour-related biliary interventionF · Interventional oncology · 2.2.5.3.2›
Biliary drainage/stenting specifically integrated with oncologic goals, future liver remnant and systemic/surgical treatment.

Indications

  • Malignant obstruction causing cholangitis, symptomatic jaundice or preventing planned surgery/systemic therapy.

Contraindications / cautions

  • Drainage that will not improve symptoms, treatment eligibility or infection control; indiscriminate drainage of atrophic/nonfunctional segments.

Work-up

  • Define hilar level, lobar atrophy and functional liver; coordinate with HPB surgery/oncology before crossing ducts.
  • LFT/bilirubin, infection, renal/coagulation; biliary cultures/antibiotic plan.

Planning & technique

  • Drain functional liver that needs decompression; minimise contrast contamination of ducts that cannot be drained.
  • Choose unilateral/bilateral, external/internal and stent configuration according to anatomy and treatment plan.

Immediate post-care

  • Sepsis and drain/stent function monitoring.

Follow-up

  • Bilirubin response and systemic/surgical treatment readiness; manage recurrent obstruction.

Complications & rescue

  • Cholangitis, haemobilia, bile leak, stent occlusion/migration, liver abscess.

EBIR pearls

  • In hilar malignancy, drainage strategy should match the oncologic/surgical plan before the needle touches the liver.

On-call one-minute checklist

  1. What is the exact clinical problem and is IR the right treatment now?
  2. Haemodynamic / respiratory stability: does resuscitation, airway control or surgery need to happen in parallel?
  3. Review the actual imaging yourself; identify target, access, culprit vessel/duct and likely pitfalls.
  4. Check Hb/platelets/coagulation, renal function/electrolytes and crossmatch as relevant to urgency.
  5. Anticoagulant/antiplatelet status and reversibility; balance bleeding vs thrombosis.
  6. Infection: cultures, antibiotics and source-control urgency.
  7. Consent/capacity or emergency treatment basis; document major procedure-specific risks.
  8. Anaesthesia/sedation, monitoring and analgesia plan.
  9. Equipment and bailout: embolics, covered stent, larger sheath, drain, chest tube, resuscitation support.
  10. Post-procedure destination and observations: ward vs HDU/ICU, neurovascular checks, Hb/LFT/renal tests, imaging.
  11. Explicit follow-up / ownership: who checks the drain, stent, pathology, bilirubin, wound, Doppler or response scan?
